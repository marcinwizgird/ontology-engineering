Natural language to SQL: what works in 2026, and where ontologies earn their keep

A baseline for IT architecture and IT leadership. It sets out what the research and benchmarks actually show, which gains are cheap and proven, what accuracy to plan for, and how SIP's ontology and knowledge-graph building blocks fit in.

Six points to take away

Context beats models

Natural-language querying works well wherever the business meaning of the data is already written down, and poorly wherever it is not. The model matters less than the semantics you give it.

83% vs 15–50%

Leaderboards are not your data

The best public score on BIRD is 82.95% (humans: 92.96%). On realistic enterprise benchmarks the best systems reach 15–50%, and close to 0% on real warehouses (BEAVER).

+17–40 points

Curated semantics are the biggest lever

Adding written-down business meaning (a glossary, a semantic layer, an ontology with mappings) lifts accuracy by 17–40 points. Within the frontier tier, the choice of model was not statistically significant.

0 silent errors

Formal semantics change how systems fail

Checkable semantics turn plausible wrong numbers into explained refusals. One 2026 study reported 97.4% correct with zero wrong-but-executed answers, against 55.3% for direct SQL generation.

53% correct or close

Production today is a copilot

LinkedIn's SQL Bot is rated 53% correct-or-close by experts; Uber cut query authoring from about 10 to 3 minutes. Successful deployments help SQL-literate users and do not yet replace them.

0% vs 70–100%

Ontologies win on multi-hop questions

A metric-style semantic layer scored 0% on questions that need several entity hops, where it could not express the path. Graph-shaped semantics target exactly these role- and path-sensitive questions.

11–43 questions

The ontology evidence is thin

Every head-to-head number for ontology or semantic-layer approaches comes from one small, mostly vendor-run insurance benchmark. Measuring on our own data is the most valuable thing we can do.

01 · The state of the art

Public benchmarks are near their ceiling; enterprise tasks are not

Clean academic benchmarks have plateaued, gaining 1–2 points a year at the top since late 2024. Benchmarks built from real enterprise conditions (large schemas, company-specific terms, dialects, ambiguous questions) score far lower. The failures there are about knowledge and context, not SQL syntax.

Differences under about 3 points at the top of a leaderboard mean nothing, so leaderboard rank should not drive vendor or model selection.

02 · What actually moves accuracy

Grounding and checking beat clever generation

Single-paper ablations give a consistent ranking. The large gains come from giving the model business meaning and real examples. Repair, voting and selection add a few points each. Several popular techniques show no gain with frontier models, and pruning the schema tightly makes results worse.

03 · With and without ontologies

Every study shows a large gain from curated semantics

The same pattern repeats across ontologies, semantic layers and even a plain markdown description: once the business meaning is supplied, accuracy jumps. What no study shows yet is an ontology beating a well-built semantic layer, or the reverse, on accuracy alone. The gain comes from having curated semantics at all. The formalism decides what else you get: checkability, reasoning, multi-hop paths and reuse.

04 · The risk that matters

The real cost is the confident wrong number

A query that runs and returns a plausible but wrong figure is worse than no answer, because nobody notices. Accuracy alone hides this. What separates systems for regulated use is how they fail: a validator that checks queries against the ontology converts many silent errors into explained refusals.

Anchors: Level 0 from EntSQL, Falcon and LiveSQLBench; level 1 from Cube (+17–23 pp) and dbt raw schema (64.5%); level 2 from Databricks Genie (77% after examples), LinkedIn (53% correct-or-close) and Genie promotion gates (60/70/80%); level 3 from SPC (97.4%), dbt semantic layer (98–100%) and ontology query check (72% + 8% refusals with a 2023 model).

✓Realistic to promise

  • A copilot that drafts SQL for analysts and shows its interpretation, cutting authoring time by half or more.
  • 80–95% correct on a scoped domain once its semantics are curated and examples verified, measured on our own questions.
  • Explained refusals instead of wrong numbers when a question falls outside the governed vocabulary.
  • Semantics written once and reused by NL querying, BI, GraphRAG and APIs.
  • Low run cost: about US$0.01 and 3 seconds per question for the best 2026 SPARQL system.

✗Not realistic yet

  • Unattended, enterprise-wide Q&A for business users with no human review at above 90%.
  • Resolving ambiguous questions on its own: agents ask once where tasks hide four ambiguities (17% on BIRD-Interact).
  • Leaderboard scores carrying over to our schemas, or a newer model fixing missing semantics.
  • An ontology that pays off on simple lookups: frontier text-to-SQL already handles those.
  • Security enforced in the prompt: prompt-to-SQL injection is only stopped by database permissions.

08 · The business case

Where ontologies and knowledge graphs earn their keep, and what SIP provides

The honest case for ontologies is not "higher accuracy on simple questions". It is five things a glossary or a vendor semantic layer does not give you together: checkability, multi-hop paths, reasoning, reuse across consumers, and governance on open standards. SIP is the platform that builds, validates and governs these assets, and the NL query path consumes them.

Where the ontology does not pay off. On simple lookups over a clean schema, frontier text-to-SQL reached 64–90% in 2026 and a raw-SQL arm is a serious competitor. The ontology's defensible ground is multi-hop, role- and path-sensitive questions, refusal quality and silent-error rate, and reuse across consumers. We should position and measure it there, and not claim more.

10 · Recommendation

Measure first, then build semantics where the measurement says they pay

A staged plan with go/no-go gates keeps the investment tied to evidence. Each phase is useful on its own if we stop there.

Phase 0 · weeks 0–6

Baseline on our data

  • Pick one high-value domain and 150–300 real questions from query logs
  • Score raw text-to-SQL, a generic tool-using agent and the abstain-everything floor
  • Ship low-hanging fruit 1, 3, 5 and 6
Gate: baseline accuracy and silent-error rate known, with confidence intervals
Phase 1 · weeks 6–16

Governed semantics for one domain

  • Model the domain ontology and glossary in SIP, reusing FIBO where it fits
  • Map it to the source tables; turn on the query check and refusals
  • Compare against phase 0 arms, paired, three runs each
Gate: ≥ 70% in-scope, ≤ 5% wrong-but-executed, a clear win on multi-hop questions
Phase 2 · months 4–9

Scale and reuse

  • Extend to two or three adjacent domains on the same ontology
  • Publish to the knowledge graph; reuse in GraphRAG and data-product APIs
  • Monitor accuracy drift on the evaluation set every release
Gate: ≥ 80% in-scope for production, explained refusals out of scope

How we will measure success

Accuracy on our own questionsOver several runs and perturbed data copies, with paired confidence intervals; never leaderboard rank.
Wrong-but-executed rateThe headline risk metric: plausible answers that are wrong.
Refusal qualityScored against the trivial "abstain on everything" floor, which most published systems fail to beat.
Multi-hop accuracyMeasured separately, because this is where ontologies should differ from raw SQL.
Time to answer and adoptionAuthoring time saved and weekly active users, as Uber and LinkedIn report.
Cost per questionRecorded per call; the 2026 benchmark winner ran at about US$0.01.
